# offer — package what we sell into something people can say yes to

A good offer makes the first yes small and the value obvious. This move designs or repairs the offer: entry point, scope, outcome, price logic, risk reversal and the path to the next purchase.

## Use when

- "What should we charge?", "prospects say it's too expensive", "deals stall at proposal", "design a pilot", "productise our service", "entry offer", "upsell path".

## Reads

`COMPANY.md`, `ICP.md` (who signs, budget situation), `POSITIONING.md`, `winloss` notes on price and terms, `PROOF.md`.

## Steps

1. **Diagnose the current offer.** Where do deals stall, and what do buyers say at that point? Price too high is often scope too vague or risk too big.
2. **Define the outcome** in the buyer's terms, and how both sides will know it happened. If it cannot be measured, say so and make the deliverable concrete instead.
3. **Design the ladder:**
   - **Entry** — small, fixed scope, fast, low risk; proves value and builds the business case for the next step.
   - **Core** — the main engagement or product tier.
   - **Expansion** — what naturally follows when the core works.
   For each: who signs, typical budget authority at that level, duration, deliverables.
4. **Price logic.** Anchor to value or to the alternative's cost (from `alternatives`), not to hours. Show the reasoning; leave the final number to the person. Never invent market benchmarks; mark any figure without a source as `Believed`.
5. **Reduce perceived risk** with the lightest mechanism that works: fixed scope, clear exit, pilot-to-contract credit, references, guarantees only where the business can honour them.
6. **Write the offer in one paragraph** a buyer could forward to their boss.

## Output

The offer ladder, the one-paragraph offer, price reasoning with open questions, and how to test it (next five proposals, a landing page, a direct ask).

## Don't

- Don't promise outcomes the business can't control.
- Don't recommend discounting as the first lever.
